Abstract
Why do some social groups remain cohesive while others fragment into polarized factions?
How do individuals resist dominant norms? This paper introduces the concept of social reward
economies—structured systems of social feedback that determine which behaviors, beliefs, and
schemas are rewarded, tolerated, or punished. We develop a formal procedural framework that
models cohesion, polarization, resistance, and manipulation as emergent properties of these
dynamic reward economies.
We conceptualize cohesion as the alignment of behaviors around a dominant reward economy;
polarization as the coexistence of competing reward economies reinforcing divergent beliefs and
practices; resistance as the creation of alternative reward economies that valorize dissent; and
manipulation as the strategic distortion of reward structures to engineer desired social outcomes.
By integrating micro-level feedback processes with macro-level social structures, this frame-
work complements existing theories of norms, power, and resistance through a formal model.
It advances sociological understanding by demonstrating how social order, conflict, and change
emerge from recursive interactions among reputation-seeking agents operating within and across
competing reward economies. This perspective provides new tools for empirical research, formal
modeling, and the analysis of cohesion, polarization, and manipulation in contemporary digital
and organizational contexts.

Why do some social groups remain cohesive while others fracture into polarized factions? Why do
certain norms persist despite organized resistance, while others collapse rapidly under contestation?
And how can powerful actors strategically manipulate social systems to entrench their influence or

norms, power, conflict, and social order. Yet despite decades of rich theorizing, existing approaches

In this paper, we propose that these fundamental aspects of social life can be understood

sociology. While existing theories have long recognized that rewards and sanctions shape behavior,
they have not treated these processes as constituting an economy with its own structure, com-

by dynamic reward economies, we provide a powerful new lens for understanding how micro-level
interactions produce macro-level social phenomena.
Using this framework, we develop a feedback-based procedural model that explains cohesion,
polarization, resistance, and manipulation as emergent properties of the dynamics of social reward
economies:
• Cohesion arises when a single, dominant reward economy produces uniform incentives that
align behaviors around shared norms and practices, stabilizing group unity.
• Polarization emerges when multiple competing reward economies coexist, each reinforcing
different and often incompatible behaviors or beliefs, leading to subgroup fragmentation.
• Resistance is conceptualized as the emergence of an alternative reward economy that counters
the dominant system. When prevailing feedback structures fail to incentivize compliance—due
to moral conviction, solidarity, or strategic recalibration—actors co-create new systems of
rewards and sanctions that valorize dissent and foster counter-hegemonic practices.
• Manipulation occurs when strategic actors intentionally distort the reward economy—amplifying,
suppressing, or redirecting feedback signals—to engineer desired patterns of behavior, alle-
giance, or belief within the group.

1.2 The Social Reward Economy: A Synthesis of Our Procedural Models

work. In our paper Social Rewards and Recursive Feedback: A Procedural Model of Norm Emer-

While each preceding paper detailed specific micro-level mechanisms and their direct macro-

that addresses the complex, evolving nature of social order, conflict, and change.

1.3 A New Tool for Sociological Inquiry
We argue that the concept of a social reward economy provides a powerful new foundational tool
for sociological inquiry. This framework shifts analytical focus from static traits or exogenous struc-
tures to the dynamic distribution, contestation, and manipulation of rewards and sanctions that
constitute social life. It complements and extends existing theories by formalizing the microfoun-
dational mechanisms underlying cohesion, polarization, resistance, and manipulation.
In doing so, our approach advances sociological understanding of how social order, conflict, and
change emerge from everyday feedback dynamics, opening new pathways for empirical research,
formal modeling, and theoretical synthesis across sociology, political science, and organizational

for advancing sociological theory:
1. Conceptually Clear and Precise: It defines each construct—social reward economies, feedback

2 Social Feedback and the Dynamics of Resistance, Cohesion, Po-
larization, and Manipulation
The dynamics of social influence, norm formation, and power have long been central to sociological
inquiry. Classical theories offer powerful accounts of how norms are maintained and authority
exercised, yet they often leave underdeveloped the procedural mechanisms by which resistance
emerges, how social feedback can be strategically distorted, and how these same dynamics drive
the formation of cohesive groups and broader patterns of polarization. This review synthesizes key
literature on resistance, manipulation, and group cohesion and polarization, and shows how our
feedback-driven model complements and extends these traditions by formalizing the micro-level
processes that generate macro-level normative and structural outcomes.
2.1 Resistance and the Social Reward Economy
Resistance, often understood as the rejection, contestation, or reconfiguration of dominant in-
terpretive authority, is a multifaceted phenomenon. Sociological theory has explored its diverse
manifestations, from overt rebellion to subtle subversion.
Bourdieu’s framework, particularly his concepts of habitus and doxa (Bourdieu, 1990, 2000),
offers a powerful account of how domination is reproduced as actors internalize the taken-for-
granted rules of a social field. While Bourdieu acknowledges potential misalignments—such as
through hysteresis or cross-field dissonance—his theory provides limited insight into how such
tensions evolve into organized opposition or how micro-level interactions aggregate into field-level
normative transformation. Resistance often appears as a residual effect rather than a dynamically
modeled process.
In contrast, James C. Scott’s work on hidden transcripts (Scott, 1990) and Michel de Certeau’s
theory of everyday tactics (de Certeau, 1984) illuminate how subaltern groups develop oppositional
discourses and practices outside direct surveillance. These accounts highlight resistance as dis-
persed, improvised, and often covert—not a matter of open revolution but of everyday practices
that enact viable alternatives within dominant systems. Charles Tilly’s concept of contentious per-
formances (Tilly, 2006) further emphasizes that both domination and resistance are performative
and contingent, shaped by institutional context and historical repertoires.

Contemporary institutional theories such as Strategic Action Field (SAF) theory (Fligstein
and McAdam, 2012) view resistance as the outcome of field-level crises and strategic maneuvering
by challengers and incumbents. While SAF highlights exogenous shocks and collective action
dynamics, it tends to focus on organizational and elite actors, offering less insight into how ordinary
individuals experience and enact resistance through everyday feedback processes. Similarly, framing
theory (Snow and Benford, 1988) explains the success of social movements in terms of diagnostic and
prognostic frames that rearticulate personal experiences as collective grievances. These discursive
mechanisms are powerful, but they often abstract away from the underlying interactional dynamics
that drive behavioral adaptation and norm change.
Feminist standpoint theory emphasizes that marginalized groups possess epistemic privilege
grounded in lived experience, and thus resistance often takes the form of challenging dominant
epistemologies (Collins, 1990). While these insights foreground structural injustice and epistemic
asymmetries, they typically do not model the interactional or procedural dynamics through which
resistance gains traction. Likewise, network diffusion models (Centola, 2010) conceptualize resis-
tance as a contagion process, emphasizing threshold effects and local reinforcement. These models
capture important tipping dynamics but abstract away from the content of norms, the weighting
of social feedback, and the formation of counterpublic evaluative structures.
Across these approaches, resistance is typically theorized through discourse, structural con-
tradiction, or diffusion processes. What remains underdeveloped is a formal, microfoundational
account of how resistance emerges from dynamic social interactions—how localized behavioral de-
viations attract reinforcing feedback, gain reputational traction, and crystallize into alternative
normative orders.
Our approach addresses this gap by conceptualizing resistance as the endogenous emergence of
an alternative social reward economy —a system of feedback-based incentives and sanctions that
valorizes behaviors and beliefs contrary to the dominant norm. Using a procedural model grounded
in micro-level feedback dynamics, we show how misalignments between dominant norms and local
interests can trigger feedback recalibrations. As alternative behaviors begin to attract approval
or reinforcement, recursive social feedback loops can coalesce into coherent subgroups governed by
a distinct reward economy. This framework formalizes how resistance arises not only from moral
conviction or strategic calculation, but from the accumulation of socially distributed feedback
signals that reconfigure the evaluative structure of a group. It enables both generative modeling
and empirical analysis of resistance as a process of norm formation, competition, and change.
2.2 Cohesion and the Dynamics of Social Feedback
Cohesion—the forces that bind individuals into stable social groups—has been a central concern in
classical and contemporary sociology. Durkheim, in particular, conceptualized social cohesion as
the moral and normative glue that maintains solidarity within societies (´Emile Durkheim, 1893). In
The Division of Labour in Society , he distinguishes between mechanical and organic solidarity: the
former arising from homogeneity and shared values, the latter from interdependence in complex

societies. In both forms, cohesion is maintained by shared normative structures that generate
alignment and reduce deviance.
Our model builds on this foundational insight by specifying how such alignment is procedurally
maintained through feedback dynamics within what we term social reward economies . In this
framework, individuals are modeled as socially responsive agents who seek to maximize social
rewards—such as approval, prestige, and status—through behavior and belief alignment. Cohesion
emerges when feedback is consistently reinforcing, guiding agents toward behavioral convergence
and shared normative schemas.
Social network theory emphasizes homophily—the tendency of individuals to associate with
similar others—as a primary driver of network cohesion (McPherson et al., 2001; Granovetter,
1973). Our model reframes this in terms of selective feedback weighting: agents disproportionately
value feedback from similar or high-status peers, which accelerates within-group alignment and
subgroup consolidation. Homophily thus becomes not merely a static structural feature, but a
dynamic, reward-driven optimization strategy.
Theories of identity, such as Social Identity Theory (Tajfel and Turner, 1979), underscore the
importance of group membership for individual self-concept and emotional well-being. Our model
extends these insights by specifying the recursive feedback mechanisms that reinforce identity:
agents receive positive reinforcement for behaviors aligned with group norms and negative or de-
weighted feedback for deviation. This creates durable group boundaries and normatively coherent
subgroups, which reflect Durkheim’s idea of moral integration at a smaller scale.
Together, these dynamics help explain how cohesive communities emerge and persist. By con-
ceptualizing cohesion as a product of convergent reinforcement in structured feedback environments,
our model formalizes the microfoundations of group solidarity, bridging Durkheimian theory with
contemporary network and identity research.
2.3 Polarization and the Dynamics of Social Feedback
While cohesion stabilizes behavior within groups, it also sets the stage for polarization across
groups. Polarization refers to the process by which distinct social groups develop increasingly
divergent beliefs, values, and identities—often accompanied by mutual hostility or distrust. Our
model offers a procedural explanation of this phenomenon as the outcome ofcompeting social reward
economies.
Communication studies have documented the rise of echo chambers and filter bubbles (Pariser,
2011; Sunstein, 2001), where individuals are exposed primarily to confirmatory information. These
patterns are often attributed to technological design or media fragmentation. However, our model
shows how they can arise endogenously: agents, in seeking to maximize social rewards, preferen-
tially attend to and reinforce feedback that validates their existing schemas. This leads to infor-
mational homogenization within groups and the entrenchment of mutually divergent worldviews
across groups.
Political science and media studies have further identified the growth of affective polariza-

tion—deep emotional and moral divisions tied to group identities (Iyengar and Prior, 2010; Leven-

while de-legitimating others, epistemic fragmentation increases and cross-group trust collapses.
The pursuit of social rewards thus drives not only within-group cohesion but also between-group

Sociological accounts of identity, while highlighting in-group favoritism, often under-specify the

level patterns of polarization. It shows how the very dynamics that produce cohesion can, under
conditions of fragmented attention and status competition, lead to division. Polarization is thus
not merely a failure of consensus but a patterned outcome of interacting social reward economies.
2.4 Strategic Distortion in the Social Reward Economy
The strategic distortion of social feedback constitutes a potent and increasingly prevalent form
of power, particularly in contemporary digital environments. Existing sociological theories offer
various lenses through which to understand manipulation, propaganda, and engineered consensus.
Symbolic interactionism, particularly Goffman’s theory of dramaturgy (Goffman, 1959), inter-
prets manipulation as the strategic management of impression and the construction of social reality
through performance. Actors curate appearances to influence how situations are defined and in-
terpreted. Contemporary phenomena such as fake news amplification and bot-generated likes or
shares can be understood as ”team performances” that manufacture a collectively sustained fa¸ cade
of legitimacy. However, interactionist accounts often lack formal mechanisms for how such micro-
level performances scale into macro-level belief systems or contribute to norm diffusion within larger
social systems.
Gramsci’s theory of cultural hegemony (Gramsci, 1971) conceptualizes manipulation as part of a
broader hegemonic strategy in which dominant groups secure ideological consent. Misinformation
and cultural production serve to naturalize particular worldviews and marginalize alternatives.
While this perspective powerfully foregrounds structural domination, it tends to treat manipulation
as a consequence of institutional power rather than a manipulable process of social feedback that
dynamically reshapes perceived legitimacy and behavioral incentives.
Herman and Chomsky’s propaganda model (Herman and Chomsky, 1988) similarly identifies
mass media as a filtering system that prioritizes elite interests through agenda-setting and framing.
This model underscores institutionalized manipulation but offers limited insight into how individu-
als adapt behavior in response to strategically distorted feedback, or how such distortions can lead
to the emergence of insular, ideologically cohesive groups.
Network diffusion and threshold models (Watts, 2002) explain the spread of misinformation via

over adoption tipping points, enabling rapid diffusion. These models effectively capture nonlinear

Critical media theories (Couldry and Hepp, 2017) emphasize how digital platforms and algo-

While closely aligned with our perspective in highlighting structural affordances, these theories typi-

Across these literatures, manipulation is commonly framed as an artifact of structure, discourse,
or platform design. What remains underdeveloped is a procedural and microfoundational account
of how feedback signals themselves—when strategically distorted—reconfigure the evaluative envi-
ronment in which agents adapt their behavior. Our model addresses this gap by conceptualizing
manipulation as the intentional restructuring of the social reward economy: the system of feedback-
based incentives through which norms and behaviors are evaluated, reinforced, or suppressed.
By modeling agents as reflexive, feedback-seeking entities, our procedural framework shows how
manipulation can inflate the perceived reward value of particular beliefs or behaviors, even in the
absence of genuine support. Artificial amplification—via bots, coordinated actors, or algorithmic
curation—can manufacture the appearance of consensus, triggering behavioral adoption and norm
diffusion within the group. Over time, this distorted feedback loop stabilizes into a manipulated
reward economy, capable of producing cohesion, polarization, and belief entrenchment around man-
ufactured values.
This perspective reveals the dual nature of feedback: it functions both as a mirror of collective
valuation and as a lever of cultural engineering. It formalizes manipulation not simply as miscom-
munication or distortion, but as a recursive process by which the incentive structure of social life
is strategically reshaped.

3.1 Socially Responsive Agent
A common theme uniting our previous work on the emergence of behavioral norms (Jiang and
Marbach, 2025c), common schemas (Jiang and Marbach, 2025b), and the role of power and influence
asymmetries in these processes (Jiang and Marbach, 2025a), is the dynamic interplay between
socially responsive agents—individuals who observe, care about, and adapt their behavior and
cognitive schemas based on the social responses their actions elicit—and social structure. Figure 1
illustrates this: agent A performs an action observed by agent B; agent B then provides a social
response indicating approval (encouragement) or disapproval (discouragement) of A’s action; and
agent A uses this feedback to adapt its subsequent behavior and cognitive schemas that led to
this action. This feedback loop echoes Giddens’ concept of reflexivity, a connection we build upon
in (Jiang and Marbach, 2025c,b,a) to develop a reflexivity model characterizing socially responsive
agents.
The key concepts of the resulting model are:
1. Social Situations and Social Actions: Agents encounter social situations in which they interact
with other agents, and must choose among multiple possible actions, often under uncertainty.
For example, when approaching an elevator, one must decide whether to step in first or yield.
Let S represent the set of social situations, and for each s ∈ S, A(s) is the set of possible
social actions, where we define a social action as an action that impacts other agents.
2. Schemas: Schemas are internal cognitive structures that organize perception, interpretation,
and action. In cognitive psychology, schemas are often treated as personal knowledge struc-
tures or mental templates that guide individual understanding and memory (Bartlett, 1932;
Rumelhart, 1980). These personal schemas help agents efficiently process information, antic-
ipate outcomes, and reduce cognitive load in familiar situations. Here, we focus specifically
on schemas activated in social situations—that is, schemas that precede and inform a social
action taken by an agent within such a context.
Formally, let Σ(s) denote the repertoire of schemas that an agent might draw upon to interpret
and respond in a social situation s, and let σ ∈ Σ(s) denote a schema activated by agent i in
situation s.
For most of this work, our focus is on how agents select from the repertoire Σ( s) available
in situation s, not on the origins of these schemas (Leschziner and Brett, 2021)—a question
better addressed by structural and cultural theories. In this sense, our model provides the
engine for schema selection and reinforcement through interactional success, but this engine
operates within a broader cultural landscape—the fuel and roadmap—that includes higher-
order schemas, norms, and institutional structures shaping which actions are socially legible
or desirable. While not the focus of our model, these structures constrain which schemas
circulate, what counts as a meaningful outcome, and how reinforcement patterns unfold.
This points to a multi-layered system in which interactional dynamics and cultural structures
are analytically distinct yet mutually constitutive.

However, in our discussion of resistance in Section 6, we treat resistance as an alternative
social reward economy that introduces a new set of schemas, thereby enlarging the available
repertoire Σ( s). In this case, we model the enlargement of the schema repertoire and how
agents explore this new repertoire, but not the process by which it is created—a question,
again, better addressed by structural and cultural theories. This division is analytical: in
practice, schema creation and diffusion often co-occur. Our model complements, but does
not replace, theories of cultural innovation.
3. Schema Repertoire: Agent i’s schema repertoire is a mapping Oi of the social schemas that
the agents uses across multiple interpretative situations. The schema repertoire of agent i
across all situations is denoted by Oi, where Oi(s) indicates the schema agent i activates in
situation s.
4. Perceived Benefit: The activated schema Oi(s) in a social situation s guides the selection of
a social action a performed by agent i. Agent j, observing the social action a, interprets and
evaluates it, producing a perceived benefit or rewardbj(a, s). Because the social action a stems
from the schema σ, we treat the perceived benefit bj(a, s) of the social action as a proxy for
the perceived benefit bj(σ, s) of the underlying schema σ. This approach maintains analytical
clarity while centering schemas as the cognitive-cultural drivers of behavior and feedback in
social situations. The perceived benefit bj(σ, s) can be positive or negative, reflecting agent
j’s evaluative alignment or disalignment with the schema σ activated by agent i.
5. Social Feedback: Social actions can impact other agents differently. For instance, holding the
door open might be seen as helpful or patronizing depending on the context. For action a
by agent i in situation s, the perceived benefit to agent j is denoted by bj(a, s), which can
be positive or negative. If agent i performs action a and agent j perceives it positively, they
encourage its repetition. Conversely, negative feedback discourages it. Feedback from agent j
to agent i is denoted by f(bj(a, s)), which can be positive (approving/encouraging the action)
or negative (disapproving/discouraging the action).
6. Social Feedback as Social Reward: A core tenet of our framework is that agents interpret
the social feedback of others as social rewards—valenced signals reflecting how their actions
are perceived by others. Neurocognitive studies show that positive social feedback activates
reward-related brain regions such as the striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Izuma
et al., 2010; Ruff and Fehr, 2014), while social rejection engages pain-related areas (Eisen-
berger et al., 2003). This suggests social feedback is processed similarly to material rewards.
Furthermore, social rewards shape cognitive schemas through reinforcement learning: agents
adjust their schemas based on discrepancies between expected and actual feedback (Behrens
et al., 2007), consistent with operant conditioning findings that approval reinforces existing
schemas while disapproval weakens them (Skinner, 1953).

3.5 Emergence of a Power Structure
Within this procedural model, different agents possess varying levels of influence, quantified through
their “influence weights,” which signify the degree to which an agent’s feedback and actions are
valued and considered by others. Agents with higher influence weights have a greater impact on
the group’s norms and the schemas of other agents. However, crucially, this weight is not pre-
assigned, or already existing, but it is the outcome of interactions among agents. In particular, as
discussed in (Jiang and Marbach, 2025a), this weight emerges procedurally from two complementary
processes. First, agents who consistently choose actions yielding high social benefits for others are
perceived as knowledgeable about which schemas generate desirable outcomes. This demonstrated
success signals expertise and credibility, prompting others to assign them higher feedback weights.
Second, agents who provide accurate evaluative feedback that correctly identifies and rewards
beneficial schemas in others enhance their reputation as reliable evaluators, further increasing their
influence. Thus, influence arises not merely from the possession of traits or resources, but from
their demonstrated practical value within social interactions. In sociological terms, power emerges
from practical epistemic authority rooted in everyday schemas and evaluation.
4 The Social Reward Economy
Building on the procedural model of socially responsive agents, we formalize the broader conceptual
framework of the social reward economy. This economy emerges from recursive interactions among
a group of agents who continuously adapt their schemas and behaviors in pursuit of social rewards
(or valuations)—a dynamic, context-sensitive form of social feedback that may include approval,
recognition, influence, status, or indirect material and instrumental benefits conferred by others
within relevant social groups.
More specifically, a social reward economy is defined as follows:
• A social reward economy consists of a group of socially responsive agents who interact with
each other, each agent aiming to maximize the perceived social rewards they receive from
the group by choosing schemas and behaviors that elicit positive—and avoid negative—social
responses from other agents within their specific social contexts.
This refined concept of a social reward economy underpins the procedural models and highlights
core dynamics that give rise to social structure, while explicitly acknowledging complexity and
contingency:
1. Competition for Social Rewards: Agents continuously seek to maximize contextually defined
positive feedback from others—such as approval and recognition—making social rewards a
central motivational currency. This competition drives ongoing adaptation of behavior and
schemas.

While social rewards primarily operates through symbolic rewards (e.g., social approval),
these may indirectly enable access to material resources, as we discuss in (Jiang and Marbach,

2. Emergent Structures from Decentralized Interaction: While agents’ pursuits of social rewards
are often localized and uncoordinated, their recursive adaptations lead to the emergence and
stabilization of group-level structures. Agents adjust their behavior and schemas based on
the feedback they receive, which in turn shapes the feedback others provide. This process
gives rise to emergent outcomes such as:
• Behavioral norms that guide appropriate action within specific contexts (Jiang and Mar-

• Common interpretive schemas that structure shared understanding and pathways to

• Power hierarchies grounded in reputational influence and the weight afforded to feedback
from high-status agents (Jiang and Marbach, 2025a).
While structures in a social reward economy emerge in a decentralized manner, they are mediated by
field-specific logics and pre-existing inequalities that constrain whose feedback carries weight (Bour-

strategic manipulation of social feedback by powerful actors, which may significantly shape the
dynamics and outcomes of the social reward economy, as we discuss in Section 8.
4.1 Structural Properties within the Social Reward Economy
The dynamics driving the emergence of social structures—such as behavioral norms and shared
schemas—have specific structural properties, including opinion leaders, power structures, and gossip
networks. These properties are elaborated in (Jiang and Marbach, 2025c,b,a) and summarized in
Section 3. Thus, when we refer to a social reward economy, we mean not only the general concept
of a system of socially responsive agents pursuing social rewards and the core dynamics introduced
above, but also the structural properties that emerge from these dynamics:
1. Opinion Leaders / Influential Agents: Opinion leaders whose schemas and behaviors are imi-
tated by other agents, and influential agents whose feedback carries disproportionate influence
within the group. Their influence arises from accumulated social rewards that lead to a high
reputational standing (status) within the group.
2. Gossip Networks: Gossip networks through which agents share reputational information
about others in the social group. These networks can both stabilize and destabilize power
structures by either reinforcing current opinion leaders or identifying novel opinion leaders
who have higher overall reputations.

Together, these elements provide a comprehensive account of how social order and structure

4.2 Applicability Across Diverse Social Groups
A pertinent question arising from the concept of a social reward economy is its scope of applicabil-
ity: to what type of social groups does this concept apply? We contend that the procedural model
of social reward economies offers a lens through which to understand the dynamics of virtually of
a wide range of collective, from intimate dyads to large-scale organizations and diffuse online com-
munities. The fundamental mechanism – that agents are inherently responsive to social feedback
and tend to pursue actions and interpretations that yield positive social rewards – is a broadly
applicable principle of human sociality.
While the core mechanism has broad applicablity, its salience, complexity, and specific mani-
festations will naturally vary across different types of social groups.
• Varying Intensity and Form of Rewards: In highly formalized or competitive environments,
such as professional organizations, academic fields, or online platforms, the pursuit of social
rewards (e.g., promotions, citations, ”likes,” influence scores) may be explicit and intensely
competitive. The currency of feedback might be highly structured and quantified. Conversely,
in more intimate groups like families or close friendships, the reward economy still operates,
but the rewards are often more subtle (e.g., emotional affirmation, shared understanding,
mutual support, belonging) and the feedback mechanisms less overt, relying on nuanced cues
and implicit understandings.
• Complexity and Relaxed Assumptions: Our initial models, particularly those focusing on
behavioral norms and shared schemas, began with simplifying assumptions such as egalitarian
feedback, truthful gossip, and the emergence of a single opinion leader. These idealizations
were methodologically deliberate, designed to clarify the foundational procedural mechanisms.
However, as demonstrated in subsequent papers within this quartet, the framework is robustly
extended to incorporate real-world complexities. This includes:
– Asymmetrical Influence: The introduction of ”feedback weight” formalizes how some
agents’ feedback carries disproportionate influence, reflecting existing power hierarchies
and the uneven distribution of reputational capital.
– Strategic Manipulation: The model accounts for instances where feedback is deliber-
ately distorted, manufactured, or suppressed by strategic actors, particularly relevant in
mediated digital environments.
– Conflicting Economies: The framework explicitly models how groups can fragment into
multiple, often antagonistic, social reward economies, each reinforcing divergent norms
and beliefs, leading to phenomena like polarization and organized resistance.

• An Analytical Lens, Not a Deterministic Blueprint: It is crucial to understand the social
reward economy as a powerful analytical lens rather than a deterministic blueprint for every
social interaction. It highlights a core, dynamic interplay between individual agency and
collective structure – the continuous process of seeking and providing social validation – that
shapes collective life. While agents possess mixed motivations beyond pure reward-seeking,
our framework identifies the social reward economy as a dominant and pervasive mechanism
through which social order, conflict, and change emerge.
In essence, the concept of a social reward economy provides a versatile and robust theoretical
tool for dissecting the underlying dynamics of social groups, offering a mechanistic explanation for
how diverse micro-level interactions contribute to the formation, maintenance, and transformation
of macro-level social phenomena across a wide spectrum of social contexts.
However, it is important to note that this model is not best suited for situations where social
feedback does not play a crucial role, such as in groups held together primarily by coercion or
purely material necessities. In such contexts, where compliance is enforced externally rather than
emerging through interactions within a social group, the explanatory power of a social reward
economy framework becomes limited.
4.3 Subconscious Dynamics in Social Reward Economies
A crucial distinction between a conventional market economy and our conceptualization of a social
reward economy lies in the assumed nature of agent decision-making and feedback provision. While
traditional economic models often posit agents as making deliberate, conscious calculations to
optimize their utility, our framework acknowledges a more complex, often subconscious, dynamic
at play within social reward economies.
• Subconscious Schema and Behavior Adaptation (Habitus and Dual-Process Models): In a so-
cial reward economy, a significant portion of agents’ activities, particularly in their ”crystal-
lization” phase, are governed byhabitus (Bourdieu, 1977) and operate through rapid, intuitive
processes akin to those described in dual-process models of cognition (e.g., Kahneman, 2011).
Agents react to social feedback and update their schemas behavior, even deeply ingrained,
subconscious patterns, without necessarily being consciously aware of the optimization pro-
cess. This means that individuals may adjust their demeanor, conversational style, or even
their fundamental interpretive schemas in response to subtle cues of approval or disapproval,
driven by an implicit desire to align with the reward structure of their social environment,
rather than explicit deliberation.
• Reflexive and Involuntary Feedback Provision: Furthermore, the provision of social feedback
itself is often a reflexive, subconscious act. When agents perceive another’s action as beneficial
or detrimental, their reactions – whether in terms of approval or disapproval – are frequently
expressed through immediate, emotional responses that are difficult to suppress. Humans

often cannot entirely hide their emotions; a fleeting smile, a furrowed brow, a subtle nod, or
a dismissive glance can convey powerful social feedback. This means that agents frequently
provide the corresponding social feedback unwillingly or even regret having done so later,
precisely because these expressions are often automatic and pre-conscious. This involuntary
nature of emotional expression ensures a continuous, rich stream of social feedback that
constantly informs and shapes the reward economy, even outside of deliberate communication
(Dimberg et al., 2000; Ekman and Friesen, 1975).
This emphasis on subconscious processes and involuntary emotional reactions adds a critical
dimension to our understanding of social reward economies, highlighting that their operation is
not solely driven by rational calculation but is deeply embedded in the embodied, automatic, and
affective dimensions of social life.
5 Social Reward Economy and Sociological Inquiry
Having introduced the concept of a social reward economy, the following questions naturally arise.
How useful is the concept of a socially responsive agent? Does approaching sociological questions
through this lens provide new, and meaningful, insights? Can the concept indeed be applied to a
rich set of fundamental sociological questions as it is suggested above. We explore these questions
in the following, where we use the concept of a social reward economy to sociological inquiry on
cohesion, polarization, and resistance. We argue that this concept indeed provides a useful lens the
leads to new and meaningful insights into these sociological questions. In this section, we provide
an overview of questions and findings, and provide a more in-depth discussion in the following
sections.
5.1 Social Reward Economy and Cohesion
Social groups with a salient opinion leader are characterized by a particularly focused reward
economy. Opinion leaders are individuals whose high reputation, expertise, or status within the
group renders their feedback especially influential. They serve as both behavioral models and
evaluative arbiters. Because others perceive them as reliable guides to what is valued, imitation
and alignment with their behaviors become effective strategies for maximizing social rewards.
The presence of an opinion leader produces several interrelated effects:
• Normative focus: Opinion leaders’ high-reputation behaviors are widely imitated by the gen-
eral agents so that they crystalize into group norms.
• Coherence and cohesion: As agents align themselves with the opinion leader’s behaviors
and beliefs (schemas), group members converge on shared practices, producing normative
cohesion.
20


---

## Page 21

• Reduced uncertainty: Because the opinion leader serves as a reliable referent, agents can
minimize the cognitive and social risks associated with behavioral selection.
It is important to note that opinion leaders both reflect and manufacture group consensus.
Their high reputation arises precisely because their behaviors and beliefs align with norms that are
already valued by group members, reflecting existing evaluative preferences. At the same time, by
virtue of their influence and visibility, they manufacture consensus: as others imitate and adopt
their actions or beliefs, these become further entrenched as dominant norms within the group. This
dual role mirrors Fligstein and McAdam’s (2012) concept of “skillful actors” in fields—agents who
both read and strategically shape the social environments they inhabit. Thus, opinion leaders are
not merely passive representatives of group preferences but active participants in constructing the
evaluative structures that organize social reward economies.
However, as Bourdieu (1984) and Lin (2001) note, such processes are rarely egalitarian. So-
cial rewards often accumulate disproportionately to those already advantaged, reinforcing existing
hierarchies. Opinion leaders’ epistemic authority—knowing what “sells” in the group—derives in
part from their structural position, cultural capital, and network centrality, shaping not just what
is rewarded but who is authorized to define value itself.
5.2 Diffuse Social Reward Economies in Leaderless Groups
By contrast, groups that lack a clear opinion leader exhibit a more diffuse and fragmented reward
economy. In these settings, no single individual commands widespread imitation or evaluative
authority. As a result:
• Feedback is heterogeneous, with different agents valuing different behaviors or interpretations.
• Behavior and schema adaptation proceeds primarily through distributed feedback weighting
rather than focused imitation, leading to greater diversity in behaviors and beliefs.
• The absence of a dominant normative focus produces a group that is less coherent and more
pluralistic in its evaluative structure.
While such groups may be less cohesive, they retain flexibility and openness to innovation, as
no single normative frame monopolizes the reward economy. For instance, decentralized activist
networks without clear leadership often sustain diverse tactics and goals, reflecting their diffuse
evaluative structures.
5.3 Social Reward Economy and Polarization
Finally, in some groups, multiple opinion leaders emerge, each with their own followers and norma-
tive schemas. Here, the social reward economy becomes fragmented into competing sub-economies.
Each opinion leader anchors a distinct set of behaviors and cognitive schemas that are reinforced
within their subgroup. This produces a group-level configuration characterized by:

---

## Page 22

• Multiple focal points. Each opinion leader defines a separate normative frame, generating
distinct pathways to social reward.
• Subgroup cohesion and intergroup polarization. While each subgroup develops strong internal
coherence around its leader’s norms, the broader group becomes polarized as these norms
compete for legitimacy.
• Contested social realities. Agents face incompatible reward structures, making cross-group
alignment costly and reinforcing the durability of subgroup divisions.
In such contexts, polarization is not merely a matter of differing opinions but emerges as a
structural property of the reward economy itself. Each camp offers distinct and internally coherent
rewards for alignment, producing durable factionalization within the broader group. This dynamic
is evident in contemporary political movements with multiple factional leaders, where competing
narratives structure divergent pathways to social recognition and belonging.
6 Social Reward Economy and Resistance
Resistance is not simply deviance or disobedience. It is a socially situated choice to reject or
reconfigure others’ interpretive authority, arising when interests misalign, perspectives become in-
commensurable, or compliance yields diminishing returns. Building on Foucault’s insight that
power is productive rather than merely repressive (Foucault, 1978), resistance is not the absence
of power but its reciprocal—emerging within the same relational structures that enable influence.
6.1 Resistance as an Alternative Social Reward Economy
We identify a key condition for resistance as the misalignment between dominant behavioral norms
and an actor’s perceived interests or values. When prevailing norms no longer yield desirable
outcomes, agents begin questioning their legitimacy and searching for alternatives. This moment
of dissonance—when adherence becomes costly or unrewarding—opens the door for resistance.
While Strategic Action Field theory (Fligstein and McAdam, 2012) and Scott’s (Scott, 1985)
theory of everyday resistance acknowledge such misalignment, they leave important gaps: SAF
emphasizes field-level crises and elite conflict, lacking a fine-grained account of how ordinary actors
interpret and respond to normative dissonance.
The concept of a social reward economy offers a new lens to address these limitations. Rather
than treating resistance as a product of large-scale ruptures or diffuse undercurrents, it reframes
resistance as the emergence of an alternative reward economy. Specifically, when a subgroup Gr ⊂
G perceives the dominant norm as detrimental rather than beneficial, its members can form an
alternative reward economy—one that rewards behaviors aligned with an alternative norm reflecting
their interests and needs.
This alternative social reward economy directly challenges and rivals the dominant economy. It
may contest the dominant norm in its entirety or target specific aspects of it. These two economies

without such reinforcement, misaligned actors may remain isolated or revert to conformity.
This framework thus captures the subtle, often gradual processes through which normative
shifts unfold, explaining why some misalignments remain dormant while others escalate into full-
scale challenges to the status quo.

6.3 Defiance and Alternative Reward Economies
A natural objection arises: what about religious asceticism, punk subcultures, or radical dissent,
where defiance of mainstream social rewards is precisely the point? Does this not fall outside a
reward-based framework? On closer inspection, however, even these cases are better understood
not as rejections of social rewards per se, but as participation in alternative reward economies with
distinct systems of valuation.
In such contexts, actors accrue status precisely through their explicit rejection of dominant
norms. The punk musician gains credibility by resisting commercial success, just as the ascetic
monk earns spiritual capital through material renunciation. These behaviors are not exceptions to
the social reward economy model; they are its exemplars, demonstrating how subcultures, counter-
publics, and religious orders construct self-referential systems of valuation, complete with internal
feedback loops, reputational currencies (e.g., authenticity, piety), and opinion leaders (e.g., punk
icons, spiritual authorities).
This perspective does not imply that all human action is narrowly instrumental or econom-
ically rational. Rather, it highlights that even anti-mainstream behaviors stabilize within some
system of social feedback that defines and distributes recognition. The explanatory power of the
framework lies precisely in its ability to map how disparate groups—from corporate boards to anar-
chist collectives—generate and enforce distinct reward structures that guide behavior and construct
meaning.

7 Social Reward Economy and Cohesion and Polarization
This section delves into the paradox inherent in social feedback: how the very mechanisms designed
to foster consensus within groups can, under specific conditions of selective weighting and network
segregation, generate profound intergroup divisions. We start out by exploring the case of cohesion,
and then focus on polarization.
7.1 Cohesion as a Focused Social Reward Economy
The emergence of a common social norm and the consolidation of group cohesion within our model
begin with the presence of strong opinion leaders. These are agents who, by virtue of their high
reputation, exert a disproportionate influence on others: either other agents directly imitate their
behaviors and schemas, or assign them a high influence weight ( wj). As individuals prioritize
and internalize signals from these opinion leaders, their behaviors and cognitive schemas—their
fundamental ways of interpreting the world—begin to align around the norms endorsed by these
figures. This process acts as the initial spark for a common norm, as followers increasingly adopt
and overtly reinforce the leader’s behaviors and beliefs.
As this nascent norm solidifies within the subgroup, it becomes a powerful focal point for
subsequent feedback. A self-reinforcing loop takes hold: agents preferentially weight feedback
that confirms this burgeoning shared norm, while simultaneously discounting or actively ignoring
inputs from outside the subgroup or from dissenting internal voices. This dynamic amplifies the
homogeneity of beliefs and behaviors, fostering a strong sense of internal cohesion and a shared
collective identity among members. It is through this recursive feedback that a group, initially
bound by influential figures, becomes a cohesive entity with a distinct normative center.
This dynamic leads to a highly focused social reward economy with a clear focal point indicating
which behaviors and schemas are rewarded and which are not. This focus and clarity make it easier

for members of the social reward economy to choose behaviors and schemas that lead to high
reputation—i.e., to receiving positive social feedback and rewards within the group. On the flip
side, the high focus of the social reward economy can also make the group rigid and resistant to
change.
These observations lead to an alternative definition of cohesion: a group is cohesive if it
1. has a clear norm in terms of behaviors and cognitive schemas that are perceived as highly
beneficial by group members,
2. that is adapted and followed by (most) group members,
3. leading to group members performing actions perceived as highly beneficial by the group, and
hence receiving positive social feedback and rewards.
Within our model of a social reward economy, the existence of a clear norm within the group is
facilitated by opinion leaders who embody the norm and whose behaviors and cognitive schemas
are adopted and emulated by the group. We refer to a social reward economy characterized by
such influential opinion leaders that produce the properties (1)–(3) defining cohesion as a cohesive
social reward economy.
It is important to note that the presence and influence of opinion leaders are critical: without
sufficiently prominent or respected leaders, the formation of a clear, shared norm may falter, result-
ing in fragmented or weakly cohesive groups. Conversely, highly focused social reward economies
led by strong opinion leaders can foster robust cohesion but may also risk rigidity and resistance
to change. Thus, understanding the role of opinion leaders within the social reward economy
is key to explaining both the emergence and stability of group cohesion, as well as its potential
vulnerabilities.
7.1.1 Internal Power of a Cohesive Social Reward Economy
Once a cohesive social reward economy emerges, the resulting group begins to exert significant
normative power over its members. This power is not overtly coercive; rather, it operates through
subtle yet potent mechanisms of social recognition and reward. Positive feedback is consistently
directed toward behaviors that conform to the group’s established norm, thereby validating and
encouraging compliance. Conversely, deviations from the norm receive diminished or negative feed-
back, signaling disapproval. This patterned distribution of feedback shapes individual motivations,
gently but firmly guiding members toward behavioral alignment and sustaining the group’s in-
ternal consistency. Through this feedback-driven process, the collective maintains its coherence
and reinforces its shared identity, ensuring that individual actions align with the group’s collective
benefit.

7.1.4 The #MeToo Movement: Cohesion’s Complex Role in Social Change
The #MeToo movement provides a compelling contemporary case study illustrating the concept

affective resonance and widespread visibility, enabling a norm—the rejection of sexual harassment
and assault—to emerge organically across diverse networks. This phase demonstrates how norm
emergence can be propelled by broad, loosely coordinated social feedback rather than tightly knit
organizational structures.

profound institutional power through strong organizational cohesion, clear normative alignment,
and disciplined leadership. The Civil Rights Movement’s shared moral framing, rooted in Christian
ethics, nonviolence, and constitutional rights, combined with rigorous training and unified public
demonstrations, exemplifies how high cohesion facilitates effective collective action and durable
change.
This comparison reinforces a central insight of our model: while the initial emergence of social
norms may arise from diffuse, viral feedback processes, the consolidation of power and the achieve-

strategic schema alignment, and centralized opinion leadership. Thus, opinion leaders not only
shape which norms gain prominence but also influence the organizational coherence that enables a
movement to harness its collective power effectively. The divergent trajectories of #MeToo and the
Civil Rights Movement vividly illustrate how the internal structure and cohesion of a social reward

7.2 Polarization as Competing Social Reward Economies
Building on our earlier discussion of internal cohesion and alternative social reward economies,
our model conceptualizes polarization as the coexistence of multiple, often antagonistic, cohesive
social reward economies. Each constitutes a distinct system of social feedback that selectively
validates particular behaviors, beliefs, and cognitive schemas within a group while marginalizing
or sanctioning alternatives.
7.2.1 Competition between Social Reward Economies
Within each cohesive social reward economy, dense internal feedback mechanisms amplify signals
consistent with its worldview while systematically discounting opposing perspectives. This creates
structurally insulated social systems that generate strong incentives for internal conformity and
resist cross-group convergence.
These polarized subgroups engage in persistent conflicts over key domains of social life:
• Normative Hegemony: Each group strives to establish its schemas and norms as the legitimate
or dominant framework, seeking to delegitimize alternative perspectives.
• Epistemic Authority: Groups assert the truthfulness and validity of their own narratives,
experts, and information sources, often by undermining those of opposing camps.
Polarization, therefore, is not merely a failure of consensus but a stable stalemate arising from
the mutual exclusivity and comparable strength of these competing reward economies. Neither side
achieves dominance, producing an enduring pluralism characterized by antagonistic coexistence and
persistent contestation over the definition and legitimacy of social reality itself.
The power of each faction derives from its internal cohesion and its capacity to maintain an
insulated social reward economy that systematically reinforces group-specific norms and punishes
deviation. The persistent inability of polarized groups to reconcile their divides reflects a dynamic
equilibrium in which no single faction commands sufficient influence to enforce universal norma-
tive convergence. Consequently, the broader social field becomes marked by enduring pluralism,
characterized by antagonistic standoffs and competing claims to social reality.
This dynamic is analogous to competition in market economies. For example, the historical ri-
valry between VHS and Betamax formats in the video cassette market saw each cultivating its own
ecosystem of producers, distributors, and consumers, characterized by distinct technological stan-
dards and strategic alliances. While VHS ultimately gained dominance through broader adoption
by rental stores and content producers—thus attracting more users into its reward economy—both
formats coexisted for many years in a contested market equilibrium. Similarly, in polarized social
fields, competing social reward economies can persist over extended periods, each maintaining its
distinct reality while vying for broader hegemony.

Thus, for the purposes of this discussion, we define manipulation as the intentional restruc-

signals—to artificially amplify, suppress, or redirect social rewards, thereby shaping group behaviors

Real-world feedback is rarely neutral; it is often strategic, biased, or deliberately distorted,
particularly in environments marked by power asymmetries or conflicting interests. Such distortions
can reshape the trajectory of norm emergence, stabilizing suboptimal or harmful practices that
would not arise under unmanipulated evaluative processes. The ability to effectively manipulate,
and hence distort, social feedback by generating false or misleading signals constitutes an additional
source of power—one with particular significance in contemporary digital environments.
While some reward environments emerge organically through interactional clustering or field-

capacity to shape feedback loops has become increasingly concentrated. Platform moderators,
algorithm designers, and individual actors can selectively amplify certain signals, curate norms,

9 The Social Group as a Social Reward Economy
Our framework posits a fundamental re-conceptualization of social groups: rather than merely
aggregates of individuals or networks of ties, we argue that by viewing social groups through the
lens of a social reward economy, we gain significant analytical leverage in explaining the emergence,
stability, and transformation of collective social phenomena.
At the heart of this conceptualization lies the recursive interplay between individual actions and
collective evaluations, mediated by social rewards. Most social groups—regardless of their size or
formal structure—tend to operate, implicitly or explicitly, a system of valuation that shapes which
behaviors, beliefs, and interpretive schemas are incentivized, tolerated, or sanctioned.
In this section, we explore how this approach relates to existing sociological theories, in partic-
ular the literature on institutions.
9.1 Relationship to Existing Sociological Theories
The conceptualization of social groups as social reward economies offers an integrative framework
that resonates with, complements, and extends several established sociological theories. Rather
than supplanting existing perspectives, this approach provides a mechanistic and procedural ac-
count of how many long-standing theoretical insights manifest in dynamic social interaction.
• Symbolic Interactionism and Goffman’s Interaction Order: The social reward economy pro-
vides a procedural micro-foundation for symbolic interactionist principles. It formalizes how
meaning is continuously negotiated and constructed through interaction, where social feed-
back serves as the crucial signal for interpreting the ”definition of the situation” and the
appropriateness of actions. Goffman’s insights into impression management, deference, and
the performative nature of social life are directly operationalized: agents adjust their ”per-
formance” (behaviors and schemas) based on the anticipated or received ”audience response”
(social rewards), thereby shaping their reputation and standing within the interaction or-
der (Goffman, 1959).
• Bourdieu’s Theory of Practice, Capital, and Fields: Our model mechanistically links Bour-
dieu’s abstract concepts of capital (cultural, social, symbolic) to observable dynamics. An
agent’s accumulated social rewards (capital) translates into their reputational standing in the

omy. This provides a concrete explanation forhow symbolic capital confers power: it amplifies
the influence of an agent’s feedback, making their evaluations more consequential for others’
behaviors and schemas. The ”field” can be seen as the arena where this reward economy
operates, with its own specific ”currency” of valued behaviors and interpretations (Bourdieu,
1977).
• Giddens’s Structuration Theory: Our approach directly addresses the persistent challenge of
operationalizing Giddens’s concept of reflexivity and the recursive interplay between agency
and structure. By grounding reflexivity in reward-oriented adaptation to social feedback, we
provide the ”how” for structuration: individuals’ actions (agency) are shaped by the ”rules
and resources” (structure) of the social reward economy, and in turn, their collective, reward-
seeking actions reproduce or transform these very structures. The emergence of norms and
schemas from decentralized feedback loops offers a concrete mechanism for the ”duality of
structure” (Giddens, 1984).
• Network Theory (Diffusion of Innovations, Social Influence): While network theory excels at
describing how things spread through ties, our framework explains why certain behaviors or
schemas diffuse and who becomes influential. It introduces the motivational (reward-seeking)
and evaluative (feedback weight) layers that mediate diffusion. Network structures (e.g.,
gossip networks, imitation networks) become the conduits through which social rewards and
reputational signals flow, determining the speed and pattern of adoption. Opinion leaders are
not just central nodes but agents whose feedback carries high weight due to their demonstrated
success in navigating the reward economy (Rogers, 2003; Valente, 1996).
• Rational Choice Theory and Social Exchange Theory: Our framework shares with rational
choice and social exchange theories the premise that agents are goal-oriented and tend to
maximize desirable outcomes. However, it significantly broadens the definition of ”utility”
to explicitly center social rewards (recognition, approval, status, belonging) as primary moti-
vators, moving beyond purely material or individualistic self-interest. Unlike classic rational
choice, our model emphasizes that optimization is often non-mechanical, tacit, and shaped
by socially constructed feedback, leading to emergent collective patterns rather than just
individual equilibrium (Homans, 1961; Coleman, 1990). It formalizes how social exchanges,
particularly the giving and receiving of feedback, constitute the very fabric of the reward
economy.
• Functionalism (without Functionalist Assumptions): Perhaps one of the most striking theoret-

functionalist outcomes (e.g., stable social order, shared norms) without invoking teleological

patterns is not the result of a group-level ”need” or conscious coordination, but rather an

In essence, modeling social groups as social reward economies provides a dynamic, procedural
framework that integrates the motivational insights of exchange theory, the interactional nuances of
symbolic interactionism, the structural power dynamics of Bourdieu, the recursive logic of Giddens,
and the diffusion patterns of network theory. It offers a unified language for explaining how the
pursuit and distribution of social validation drive the complex co-evolution of individual behavior,
collective culture, and emergent power structures.
9.2 Institutions as Formalized Social Reward Economies
We contend that the concept of a social reward economy is not at odds with, but rather profoundly
complemented by, the sociological understanding of institutions. Indeed, institutions can be pre-
cisely understood as mechanisms that codify and formalize the social reward economies within a
given social group or field (North, 1990).
• Codification of Feedback Mechanisms: Institutions provide explicit structures for generating
and distributing social feedback. Unlike the often implicit and diffuse feedback in informal
interactions, institutions formalize these signals through established rules, procedures, and
metrics. Examples abound: grading systems in educational institutions, promotion criteria
and performance reviews in organizations, citation counts and journal rankings in academia,
or legal statutes and penalties in the broader societal context. These institutionalized mech-
anisms transform raw social feedback into standardized, often quantified, and officially sanc-
tioned forms of reward or sanction.
• Stabilization of Valuations: By codifying feedback, institutions stabilize the ”prices” or valu-
ations within the social reward economy. They explicitly define which behaviors, interpretive
schemas, and forms of capital are deemed ”valuable,” ”successful,” or ”legitimate” within
their domain. This formalization ensures a greater degree of consistency and predictability
in the distribution of social rewards, promoting the durability and reproduction of specific
norms and cultural patterns over time and across different cohorts of agents.
• Resource Allocation and Enforcement Power: Institutions often possess significant material
and symbolic resources, as well as the authority to enforce their codified reward structures.
This means that the feedback they provide carries substantial weight, backed by the ability to
grant or withhold tangible benefits (e.g., salaries, grants, certifications) or impose significant
costs (e.g., demotion, legal penalties, exclusion). This institutional backing amplifies the
”feedback weight” (wj) of those operating within or sanctioned by the institution.

This perspective highlights a powerful complementarity: the social reward economy describes
the underlying dynamic of social valuation and feedback, while institutions represent the formal-
ized structures, rules, and enforcement mechanisms that shape, stabilize, and often entrench that
dynamic. One explains the process of social influence, the other explains its form and durability.
However, a complete understanding necessitates acknowledging several nuances:
• Power and Contestation: Institutions are rarely neutral arbiters. Their codified reward
economies often reflect and entrench existing power dynamics and the interests of dominant
groups. The formalization process itself can be a site of intense contestation, where different
factions vie to define what is officially rewarded, thereby shaping the very structure of the
social reward economy (Michaels, 2009).
• Coexistence of Formal and Informal Economies: Institutionalized reward economies do not
negate the existence of informal ones. Within any social group, a complex interplay exists
between formal, codified rewards and informal, emergent social feedback (e.g., peer respect,
subcultural approval, gossip). Agents often navigate multiple, sometimes conflicting, reward
systems simultaneously, leading to strategic choices or internal tensions.
• Dynamic Interplay and Transformation: While institutions stabilize reward economies, they
are not immutable. The very dynamics of the social reward economy, particularly through
processes of resistance and the emergence of alternative reward systems, can exert pressure
on and ultimately lead to the transformation or creation of new institutions. This recursive
relationship underscores that institutions are both products and shapers of social reward
economies (Cleaver, 2002).
By integrating these considerations, our framework offers a more comprehensive and robust
account of how social reward economies operate within, and are shaped by, the broader institutional
landscape, further bridging micro-level social dynamics with macro-level structures.
10 Conclusion
This paper has introduced the concept of social reward economies —dynamic systems of social
feedback that govern how behaviors, beliefs, and schemas are validated, contested, and transformed.
By modeling cohesion, polarization, resistance, and manipulation as emergent properties of these
economies, we have unified disparate sociological traditions under a single procedural framework.
Our key contribution lies in explicating the micro-to-macro mechanisms through which recursive
interactions among reputation-seeking agents generate and sustain power asymmetries, normative
orders, and cultural change.
10.1 Theoretical Advancements
Using the concept of a social reward economy leads to the following novel insights:
40


---

## Page 41

1. Beyond Bourdieu’s Hysteresis: While (Bourdieu, 1977) described habitus-field ”mismatches”
as passive disjunctures, our model shows how such mismatches actively fuel resistance through
the creation of alternative reward economies (e.g., #MeToo’s recalibration of gendered feed-
back structures). This bridges structuration theory and social movement research.
2. A Mechanism for Polarization : We explain why polarization persists not as ideological dis-
agreement but as competing reward economies, each offering distinct pathways to social val-
idation (e.g., climate action vs. denialism). This complements network theories of echo
chambers by specifying their internal evaluative logics .
3. Power as Feedback Manipulation : By formalizing how actors distort feedback (e.g., algorith-
mic amplification), we reveal manipulation as a distinct form of power—one that operates
through the engineering of social reality rather than brute coercion.
10.2 Future Directions
Our framework invites empirical and theoretical extensions:
• Structural Inequality: How do pre-existing hierarchies (e.g., race, class) bias initial feedback
weights? Future work could integrate our model with field theory to examine how institu-
tionalized power shapes reward economies from their inception.
• Non-Strategic Action: While we accommodate habitus-like inertia (Section 4.4), deeper study
of non-reputational motives (e.g., moral conviction) could clarify when feedback economies
fail to explain action.
• Digital Architectures: How do platforms’ technical designs (e.g., recommendation algorithms)
artificially construct reward economies? This demands interdisciplinary collaboration with
computational social science.
10.3 Bridging Temporal Scales in Sociological Analysis
A critical direction for future research lies in exploring how the concept of a social reward econ-
omy might complement—and be integrated with—approaches from historical sociology. While the
notion of a social reward economy offers a procedural, micro-level account of how social structures
such as norms, reputations, and symbolic hierarchies emerge and evolve through recursive feedback
and valuation dynamics, historical sociology provides tools for understanding the formation and
transformation of social structures over longer time scales.
In particular, historical sociology foregrounds the role of political struggles, institutional devel-
opments, and technological shifts in shaping the initial distribution of power and resources—conditions
that often become sedimented into the implicit rules and asymmetries of social interaction. The
concept of a social reward economy, by contrast, provides a framework for modeling how these

inherited conditions are reproduced, contested, or reconfigured through everyday social feedback
dynamics.
Combining these two perspectives—micro-procedural modeling of short- to medium-term dy-
namics with historically grounded analysis of long-term structural change—could offer a promising
avenue toward bridging the temporal gap that continues to challenge sociological explanation. In
this sense, the concept of social reward economy might be a tool to help us better understand how
macro-historical legacies are enacted, reinforced, or subverted in ongoing social life.
10.4 A Foundational Tool
We do not claim that social reward economies explain all social phenomena. Rather, they offer
a foundational lens for understanding how social life becomes organized around the pursuit and
distribution of validation. By isolating endogenous feedback dynamics, this framework provides a
conceptual toolkit for analyzing how such dynamics interact with broader historical, institutional,
and technological forces.
In an era of fragmented publics and engineered forms of consensus, the framework equips scholars
to examine the procedural roots of social order, conflict, and change—and, in doing so, to better
understand the economies of recognition that structure everyday life.
